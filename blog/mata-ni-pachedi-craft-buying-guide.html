Mata ni Pachedi: Understanding the Craft Before You Buy

By Beyond Soch Editorial · 17 October 2026

Start with the sacred context, not a colour palette

Mata ni Pachedi is not simply a label for red-and-black decoration. Gujarat Tourism describes sacred textiles used behind shrines, with a central goddess and surrounding imagery made through block printing and freehand painting. It associates the tradition with the Devi Pujak community along the Sabarmati. The National Portal of India also identifies it as a textile art of Ahmedabad. Those reference points should come before a discussion of how a piece might look on a wall. Ask a maker how they describe the work’s purpose and imagery.

Let the person making the work explain their process

For a particular piece, ask which elements are printed, which are painted by hand and who completed each stage. Ask about the cloth and colour process rather than assuming that every contemporary work uses an identical recipe. A seller may be an artist, a family member, a collective or a retailer; record the role they actually describe. Avoid assigning a whole community’s history to an individual without checking. A careful product description can celebrate skill without inventing an ancestral story or claiming you witnessed work you did not see.

Agree what a commission actually includes

If you are ordering a new piece, discuss the finished size, intended use, subject and any border treatment. Ask whether the proposed composition is a new work, an adaptation or a repeat of an existing design. Clarify the making period, payment stages, delivery arrangement and what happens if the schedule changes. If you need it for an occasion, share the required delivery date before agreeing to the order. Leave the maker room to explain which changes are suitable and which conflict with the way they approach the tradition.

Request a record that travels with the textile

Ask for the maker’s preferred credit, the title or subject if one is used, the material description and the date of purchase. Keep that information with the invoice and any care instructions. If you are giving the piece as a gift, pass those details to the recipient instead of writing an unsupported story on a card. For display, ask the maker about hanging, handling, cleaning and exposure to light for the actual materials used. A general internet care tip is not a substitute for product-specific advice.

Buying an object is different from using its design commercially

A wall hanging in your home and an image on product packaging are different uses. If you want to reproduce a work in a campaign, print it on merchandise or use its details as brand artwork, discuss permission and credit explicitly with the relevant creator or rights holder. For a social feature, ask before photographing a piece closely or filming a private work area. Do not present a photograph of one artist’s work as a generic free design resource. This is a practical permission checklist, not an assessment of ownership in a particular transaction.

Support the story without claiming an endorsement

An informative feature names the work, credits the maker and explains the process using verified details. Let the artist review factual statements about their own role without converting that review into a claim that they endorse every part of your publication. If you received the work through a commercial collaboration, make that relationship clear. The contextual riverfront illustration accompanying this guide is labelled precisely because it is not a documentary photograph of the craft.
