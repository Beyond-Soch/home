A Three-Minute Local-Maker Interview: Five Questions That Reveal the Work

By Beyond Soch Editorial · 18 October 2026

Choose one person and one piece of work

A short interview becomes useful when it gives one person room to explain something specific. It does not need to summarise a whole industry. The Smithsonian’s Folklife and Oral History Interviewing Guide is a broader resource for community interviewing; the three-minute structure below is Beyond Soch’s own editorial suggestion, not a Smithsonian format. Begin by agreeing what work the guest wants to discuss and which details they are comfortable showing. A finished three-minute reel may require a longer conversation, so agree the recording time separately.

Ask five questions with room for a real answer

Try these original prompts: What do you make, and who usually uses it? Which step needs the most attention, and why? What did you have to practise before you could do that step well? What do buyers often misunderstand about this product? What should someone ask or do to choose and care for it properly? Use the answers to guide follow-ups rather than forcing the guest back to a script. A concrete example is often more revealing than asking someone to describe their passion in general terms.

Prepare the recording without staging a false story

Agree on a place and time that will not interrupt an order, customer or unsafe part of the work. Ask which tools, people and private areas may be recorded. Test the sound with a short answer before beginning. If a process cannot be filmed, let the guest explain it rather than recreating an action and presenting it as spontaneous. Confirm how the person’s name and role should appear. Do not introduce a retailer as the maker of an object unless that is what they actually do.

Use the timing plan as an editing aid

The suggested sequence below totals three minutes, but it is not a promise that every interview should fill each segment. Let the strongest explanation determine the final balance. Keep the question visible or audible when an answer would otherwise lose its meaning. If you cut out a pause, do not change the substance of what was said. Supporting shots can show the object being discussed without suggesting that the guest made something they only sell. Keep a note of the original answer for factual checking.

Suggested three-minute edit

This is an editorial example, not a recording-time commitment.

Finished editFocus
0–15 secondsName, role and what the viewer is seeing
15–50 secondsThe work: one product and who uses it
50–90 secondsThe skill: one meaningful choice or step
90–135 secondsThe learning: a difficulty or common misunderstanding
135–165 secondsThe buyer’s question: choosing or caring for the product
165–180 secondsVerified public contact and a concise close

Check names, claims and the commercial relationship

Before publication, confirm the public business name, the guest’s role, product details and the contact method they want shared. Label a recollection as their account when you have not independently established it. If the interview promotes a business through payment, gifts or another material connection, consult ASCI’s current guidance and make the relationship clear. Its FAQs explain that disclosure is not limited to cash payment. A conversation can still be engaging when the audience understands why the feature was made and what has actually been verified.

Close with a useful next step, not a guarantee

Let the guest identify the public channel through which interested viewers can learn more. Do not publish a private phone number or home-workshop address merely because it appeared in a booking message. Agree on how factual corrections should be sent after publication, and correct a material error clearly if one is found. This is a planning resource, not an announcement of a confirmed Beyond Soch guest. The strongest invitation to support local work is a clear account of the person, the product and the limits of what the interview establishes.
